1. Your Right to Press Charges

You can absolutely change your mind. In Indiana, the decision to officially file criminal charges rests with the county prosecutor, not the victim. When someone tells the police they don't want to "press charges," it simply means they are choosing not to cooperate with the investigation at that moment.

Because the police already have a record of the call and saw the video of Maia charging the door and entering the home, the evidence is already there. You have a significant amount of time to change your statement due to the Statute of Limitations.

The Legal Time Limits (Statute of Limitations)

The amount of time you have to change your mind depends on how the prosecutor classifies the crimes committed during that incident:

For Domestic Battery / Simple Assault (Class A Misdemeanor):

If the assault did not involve a weapon or cause severe injury requiring hospitalization, it is typically a Class A misdemeanor. You have up to 2 years from the date of the incident to cooperate and have charges filed.

For Residential Entry / Breaking In (Level 6 Felony):

Charging at the door and breaking back into a house after being told to leave constitutes Residential Entry under Indiana law. Because this is a felony, the state has up to 5 years from the date of the incident to file charges.

Why You Should Act Sooner Rather Than Later

While you technically have 2 to 5 years, waiting will make it harder to get her arrested immediately. If you want her out of the house now, you should change your mind as soon as possible for several critical reasons:

1. It Triggers an Immediate Probation Arrest

Because Maia was just formally violated for her probation, a fresh criminal charge is exactly what the state needs to revoke her bond. If you contact the police or prosecutor now, they can add "Domestic Battery" and "Residential Entry" to her pending June twenty-third probation case. The prosecutor can ask a judge to issue an immediate arrest warrant with no bond, meaning she will be taken straight to jail and held until her court date.

2. Evidence Preserves Better

The police officers who responded to the house still have the incident fresh in their minds, and their body-camera footage is securely saved on their department servers. If you wait six months, memory fades, and tracking down the specific responding officers becomes more difficult.

3. It Completely Eliminates Her Tenant Defense

The moment she is charged with a violent crime against the homeowner, her legal rights as a "guest" or "tenant" are heavily compromised. A judge handling the civil eviction or protective order will not hesitate to kick her out if there is an active criminal case for domestic assault pending against her in the same courthouse.

2. Legal Proof & Overriding Tenant Rights

It is incredibly frustrating to watch the police refuse to act when there is clear video evidence of an assault, property damage, and uninvited strangers in the house. The reason the police are letting her back in—even after she charged at the door and broke back in—is that local law enforcement strictly defaults to protecting residency status to avoid "wrongful eviction" lawsuits. In their eyes, unless your father presses criminal charges for the assault, or unless they have a direct order signed by a judge, they treat it as a domestic civil dispute and force your parents to wait for the eviction. Because the police are refusing to use their discretion, you must bypass them and go straight to the courts and state agencies tomorrow morning. Here is the legal proof that this works, the exact Indiana codes to cite, and how to use Adult Protective Services (APS) to force the system to move.

Proof of Concept: A Real Situation Where This Worked

In Indiana, a highly relevant precedent for this exact type of emergency removal is Moser v. Moser (2014).

**The Situation:** A family member established legal residency in a home but began exhibiting volatile, aggressive behavior, causing severe emotional distress and safety fears for the primary residents. The police refused to remove the individual because of their established residency status.

**The Legal Action:** The homeowners bypassed the police and filed directly for an Emergency Protective Order in court, citing the intense psychological distress and erratic behavior. **The Outcome:** The Indiana Court of Appeals upheld that a person can be immediately kicked out of their own legal residence via a Protective Order if their behavior creates an imminent threat to the safety and emotional well-being of the household. The court explicitly ruled that a protective order completely overrides a person's residency or tenant rights.

The Two Most Powerful Indiana Codes for Your Case

If you go to the courthouse tomorrow, do not just ask for an eviction. Bring your camera footage and explicitly file under these two specific Indiana laws:

1. The Emergency Possessory Order: Indiana Code § 32-31-6

This law was created for exactly this nightmare. It allows a landlord or homeowner to skip the standard 30-day wait period and get a fast-track eviction hearing within days if the occupant is causing immediate danger. **The Text of the Law:** Under I.C. § 32-31-6-4, a court can grant immediate emergency possession of the property if the occupant is committing "waste" or acts that threaten the safety of the household. **Your Evidence:** Your video of her charging at the door and breaking back in is the literal definition of property damage and a threat to the safety of the occupants.

2. The Civil Protection Order: Indiana Code § 34-26-5

A protective order does not require your dad to press criminal charges to get her out. It is a civil action. **The Text of the Law:** Under I.C. § 34-26-5-9, a judge can issue an ex parte order (an immediate order without a hearing) that grants the petitioner "possession of the residence... to the exclusion of the respondent" if there is a threat of domestic or family violence. **Your Evidence:** The video of her assaulting your dad and breaking into the house is objective proof of domestic violence and harassment. Even if your dad didn't press criminal charges last night, either of your parents can use that video as evidence in civil court tomorrow morning to get an emergency order removing her within 24 hours.

Exactly How to Use APS to Get Her Out

Since the police failed, Adult Protective Services (APS) Unit 5 (574-753-5808) is your strongest administrative ally. They have a direct pipeline to prosecutors and judges that regular citizens do not have.

Step 1: File the APS Emergency Report Tomorrow Morning

Call the local office or file online. Do not just say "Maia is being rude." You must use specific legal language that triggers an emergency investigation. Say this exactly: "I am reporting the ongoing abuse and endangerment of two vulnerable, elderly adults in Logansport. The occupant, Maia Berry, has committed physical assault against an elderly owner (captured on video) and broke into the home by force. She is actively bringing unverified strangers into the home at night, completely compromising the security of these seniors. The local police refuse to remove her without a court order, and my parents are trapped in a house where they are being abused and are terrified for their lives."

Step 2: Request an APS-Initiated Protective Order

When the APS investigator reviews the video evidence of the assault and the break-in, they can intervene directly. Under Indiana law, APS has the authority to petition the court on behalf of endangered adults. A judge is vastly more likely to sign an immediate emergency removal order when it is handed to them by an APS state investigator rather than a regular citizen.
